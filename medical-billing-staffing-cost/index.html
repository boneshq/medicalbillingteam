Medical billing staffing cost: in-house vs outsourced vs remote staff

Last reviewed: October 18, 2026.

"How much does it cost?" is the first question practices ask about billing help, and the honest answer is: it depends on your volume, specialty and what you're comparing. Instead of quoting price ranges we can't back up, this guide shows you how to compare three common models using your own numbers and public wage data.

The three models:

  1. In-house staff you hire and employ.
  2. Outsourced billing company that runs billing for you.
  3. Dedicated remote staff from a staffing provider, working in your systems.

For the bigger picture of roles and workflow, see our revenue cycle management staffing guide.

Model 1: In-house billing staff

What you pay

Salary is the visible part. The full cost includes:

  • Wages. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a May 2025 national median hourly wage of $23.32 for billing and posting clerks, with a mean annual wage of $51,070 (BLS OEWS). For medical records specialists, the BLS category that includes medical coders, the May 2025 median was $51,140 per year (BLS OOH). Local wages vary widely, so check your metro area in BLS data.
  • Benefits and payroll taxes. In private industry, benefits averaged 30.0% of total employer compensation costs in June 2026, and wages the other 70.0% (BLS ECEC).
  • Recruiting and onboarding. Job ads, interview time, background checks, training time before the person is productive.
  • Software and equipment. Practice management seats, clearinghouse fees attributable to the user, computer, phone, workspace.
  • Supervision. The hours a manager spends reviewing work and answering questions.

What it's good for

Direct control, on-site collaboration with providers and front desk, and deep familiarity with your practice over time.

Watch-outs

Single points of failure (one biller on vacation means nothing gets worked), recruiting difficulty, and turnover. In a 2025 vendor survey, 38% of billing professionals said they struggle to find qualified candidates and 28% cited rising wages they can't afford (Tebra).

Model 2: Outsourced billing company

How pricing usually works

Billing companies commonly charge:

  • a percentage of collections (the most common model for practices);
  • a per-claim fee; or
  • a flat monthly fee, sometimes with minimums.

We don't publish typical percentages because we haven't found a reliable public source for them, and quotes vary with specialty, volume and scope. Get at least two written quotes and compare them on the same basis.

Questions that change the real price

  • What does "collections" include? Patient payments? Capitation? Refunds netted out?
  • What is included: coding, credentialing, patient statements, patient calls, appeals, old A/R cleanup, reporting?
  • Are there setup fees, minimum monthly fees or fees for old A/R?
  • What are the contract term, termination notice and data return terms?
  • Who owns the payer relationships and portal logins?

What it's good for

Practices that want to hand off the whole function and don't want to manage billing staff at all.

Watch-outs

Less day-to-day visibility, less control over priorities, and cost that rises with your collections even if the workload doesn't.

Model 3: Dedicated remote billing staff

How pricing usually works

Staffing providers typically charge a monthly or hourly rate per full-time staff member. You direct the work in your systems; the provider recruits, employs and supports the staff.

Your costs on top of the rate

  • Management time to assign work, review output and answer questions, especially in the first month.
  • Onboarding on your procedures and payers.
  • Software seats for each user.
  • Secure remote access, such as a virtual desktop (VDI) or VPN with multi-factor authentication. See our HIPAA guide for what to require.

What it's good for

Practices and billing companies that want dedicated capacity and control without local hiring, especially for queue-based work: eligibility, prior authorization, payment posting, A/R follow-up and denials.

Watch-outs

You still own the process. If your workflows aren't documented, remote staff will struggle, just as a new local hire would. Time-zone coverage, payer contract restrictions on offshore access and HIPAA safeguards all need to be settled up front.

Worksheet: compare with your own numbers

  1. Monthly workload. Claims per month, eligibility checks per day, authorizations per week, open A/R by aging bucket.
  2. Staff needed. Roughly how many full-time people that workload requires (ask your billing lead).
  3. In-house cost per FTE. Local wage × hours + benefits (BLS: benefits ≈ 30% of private-industry compensation costs) + recruiting, software, equipment, supervision ÷ 12.
  4. Outsourced cost. Quoted percentage × average monthly collections + any minimums or add-on fees.
  5. Remote staff cost. Quoted monthly rate × FTEs + your software seats + remote access + management time.
  6. Risk adjustments. Cost of a vacancy month (unworked claims aging), turnover frequency, and the value of control and visibility to you.

Hidden costs people forget

  • Vacancies. When a biller leaves, A/R keeps aging. Some claims can hit timely filing limits; Medicare generally allows one calendar year from the date of service (CMS Claims Processing Manual, Ch. 1) and many commercial payers allow less.
  • Owner time. Physicians and managers who "just help with billing" are an expensive labor source.
  • Rework. Denials that are corrected late cost more staff time than claims done right the first time. See denial management basics.
  • Switching costs. Data migration, contract exit fees and retraining when you change models.

Which model fits?

  • Choose in-house if you have steady volume, can recruit locally and want everything on-site.
  • Choose an outsourced billing company if you want to hand off the whole function and accept less control.
  • Choose dedicated remote staff if you want control and dedicated capacity, have documented workflows, and can support secure remote access.

Many practices combine models: a local billing manager plus remote specialists for specific queues.

FAQ

How much does outsourced medical billing cost? Usually a percentage of collections, a per-claim fee or a monthly fee. Compare the fee applied to your actual collections against your fully loaded in-house cost.

What is the fully loaded cost of an in-house biller? Wages plus taxes, benefits, leave, recruiting, training, software, equipment, workspace and supervision. Benefits averaged 30% of private-industry compensation costs in June 2026 (BLS).

Are remote billing staff cheaper than in-house staff? Often the rate is lower, but compare total cost including your management time, onboarding, software and secure access.

Is a percentage-of-collections fee better than paying for staff? It depends on volume and how much control you want.

What hidden costs should I include? Vacancies, turnover, overtime, aging A/R, timely filing write-offs and owner time.

How much does outsourced medical billing cost?

Billing companies commonly price as a percentage of collections, a per-claim fee or a monthly fee. The right comparison is the fee applied to your actual collections and claim volume, compared with your fully loaded in-house cost. Ask exactly what services are included and how collections are defined.

What is the fully loaded cost of an in-house medical biller?

Wages plus payroll taxes, benefits, paid leave, recruiting, training, software licenses, equipment, workspace and supervision time. BLS data show benefits averaged 30% of private-industry employer compensation costs in June 2026, so salary alone understates the cost.

Are remote medical billing staff cheaper than in-house staff?

Often the hourly rate is lower, but you should compare total cost, including your management time, onboarding, software seats and secure remote access, against the fully loaded cost of a local hire for the same role.

Is a percentage-of-collections fee better than paying for staff?

It depends on your volume and needs. Percentage pricing scales with collections and bundles management, but you give up some control. Staffing pricing is predictable and keeps control in-house, but you manage the process.

What hidden costs should I include?

Vacancies and turnover, overtime, aging A/R while positions are unfilled, write-offs from missed timely filing limits, and the time owners or managers spend doing billing themselves.
