# Offshore medical billers: pros, cons and the safeguards to require

*By the Medical Billing Team editors · Published October 18, 2026 · Updated October 18, 2026 · [How we research and review](/about/#how-we-write-our-guides)*

**Short answer:** Offshore medical billing uses billers based outside the US. Offshore medical billers can add capacity at a lower hourly cost than local hires, and many practices and billing companies use them for A/R follow-up, eligibility, payment posting and denials. HIPAA doesn't ban offshore work, but HHS notes that overseas arrangements can raise risk and enforceability questions. Check your payer and Medicaid contracts, require a BAA and strong access controls, and include offshore access in your risk analysis.

Offshore billing teams are common in US healthcare, and they're also a source of understandable worry about data security, quality and communication. This guide covers where offshore billers fit, the real risks and the safeguards to require. It's part of our [revenue cycle management staffing](/revenue-cycle-management-staffing/) series.

## Why practices and billing companies go offshore

- **Hiring difficulty.** In a 2025 vendor survey, 38% of billing professionals said they struggle to find qualified candidates, and 28% cited unaffordable wages ([Tebra](https://www.tebra.com/theintake/getting-paid/medical-billing-pain-points-insights-solutions)).
- **Cost structure.** US billing and posting clerks had a median wage of $23.32 an hour in May 2025 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ocwage.t01.htm)), before benefits, which average 30% of private-industry compensation costs ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.nr0.htm)). Offshore staff are typically priced lower per hour, but compare total cost with our [staffing cost worksheet](/medical-billing-staffing-cost/).
- **Coverage.** A dedicated offshore person can keep queues moving when local staff are out, and some schedules cover hours your office doesn't.

## Work that suits offshore billers

- A/R follow-up and claim status checks
- Eligibility and benefits verification
- Payment posting and reconciliation
- Denial follow-up and corrected claims
- Charge entry from structured source documents
- Prior authorization submissions and follow-up (see [prior authorization specialist](/prior-authorization-specialist/))

Work that needs physical presence, such as front-desk check-in, collecting payments in person or handling paper mail, stays local.

## The risks, honestly

- **Data security.** Offshore staff access PHI from outside the US. HHS's cloud computing guidance notes that HIPAA doesn't prohibit storing or processing PHI overseas, but that doing so may raise the risks to the data and can make legal enforcement harder; covered entities should consider these risks in their risk analysis ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/health-information-technology/cloud-computing/index.html)).
- **Contract restrictions.** Some payer contracts, state Medicaid programs and government plans restrict offshore access to member data. Check yours before you start.
- **Quality and communication.** Billing depends on payer-specific knowledge and clear notes. Poor hiring or training shows up quickly in denials.
- **Time zones.** Payer phone lines keep US hours. Confirm schedules in writing.

## Safeguards to require

1. **Business associate agreement** with the provider before any access, and subcontractor BAAs where applicable ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html)).
2. **Unique logins and MFA** on every system.
3. **Secure remote access** through a virtual desktop or VPN, so PHI stays in your environment.
4. **No local downloads, printing, screenshots or USB storage**, technically enforced where possible.
5. **Company-managed, encrypted devices** and secured work locations.
6. **Role-based, minimum necessary access**, removed the same day someone leaves.
7. **HIPAA training** at onboarding and annually, with records.
8. **Background checks** and a clear acceptable-use policy.
9. **Incident response** with a defined, prompt notification timeline. Under HIPAA, a business associate must notify the covered entity of a breach without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 days after discovery ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/breach-notification/index.html)). Many contracts set a shorter window.

And remember there's no official "HIPAA certification." Ask for evidence of the safeguards above instead. Our [HIPAA guide](/hipaa-outsourced-medical-billing/) has 15 questions to ask.

## FAQ

### Is offshore medical billing legal under HIPAA?

HIPAA doesn't prohibit offshore work. HHS notes overseas arrangements may raise risk and enforcement challenges, so include them in your risk analysis, sign a BAA and check payer and Medicaid contracts for restrictions.

### What billing tasks can offshore staff do?

Common tasks include A/R follow-up, eligibility verification, payment posting, denial follow-up, charge entry and prior authorization submissions.

### How do offshore billers keep PHI secure?

Through a BAA, unique logins with MFA, secure remote access via virtual desktop or VPN, no local downloads or printing, managed encrypted devices, role-based access, training and incident response.

### Do offshore billers work US hours?

Many teams work schedules that overlap US business hours so they can call payers. Confirm the schedule in writing.

### Are offshore medical billers HIPAA certified?

No one is. There is no official HIPAA certification; HHS does not certify vendors. Ask for evidence of specific safeguards instead.
