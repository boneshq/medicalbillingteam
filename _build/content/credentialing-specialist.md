# Credentialing specialist: what the role covers and how to staff it

*By the Medical Billing Team editors · Published October 18, 2026 · Updated October 18, 2026 · [How we research and review](/about/#how-we-write-our-guides)*

**Short answer:** A credentialing specialist gets providers approved, or "credentialed," and enrolled with insurance payers, and keeps them enrolled. That means gathering licenses and documents, keeping provider profiles current, submitting applications, following up until the effective date is confirmed, and tracking re-credentialing deadlines. Until enrollment is complete, claims for that provider may be denied or delayed.

Credentialing problems don't show up as a billing error until claims start denying. A new provider who starts seeing patients before payer enrollment is finished, or an expired license nobody tracked, can stall payment for weeks. This guide covers the credentialing workflow and how practices and billing companies staff it. It's part of our [revenue cycle management staffing](/revenue-cycle-management-staffing/) series.

## Credentialing vs enrollment

People use the terms interchangeably, but they're different steps:

- **Credentialing** verifies a provider's qualifications: education, training, licenses, board certification, work history, malpractice coverage and sanctions history.
- **Payer enrollment** gets the provider added to a payer's network or program so claims can be paid, with a confirmed effective date.

Hospitals and facilities also run their own credentialing and privileging. That's a separate process, though many specialists handle both.

## The workflow

1. **Collect documents.** Licenses, DEA registration where applicable, board certification, malpractice insurance, CV and work history, and identifiers such as the NPI.
2. **Maintain provider profiles.** Many commercial payers use a shared credentialing database that providers must keep current and re-attest periodically. Medicare and Medicaid have their own enrollment systems.
3. **Submit applications** to each payer, following that payer's process.
4. **Follow up.** Track every application with a date submitted, a reference number, the payer contact and the next follow-up date. Applications stall without follow-up.
5. **Confirm effective dates and contracts.** Make sure the provider is linked to the right group, tax ID and locations, and that billing knows the effective date.
6. **Track expirations and re-credentialing.** Licenses, malpractice policies, DEA registrations and payer re-credentialing cycles all expire on different schedules.

## What slows credentialing down

- Incomplete or inconsistent applications (addresses, practice locations, dates)
- Profiles not re-attested on time
- Missing malpractice or license documents
- Applications submitted late, after the provider has already started
- No follow-up after submission
- Billing not told the effective date, so claims go out too early or too late

Timelines vary widely by payer, state and application type, so plan well ahead of a provider's start date. Ask each payer for current processing expectations rather than relying on a general number.

## A simple credentialing tracker

Even a spreadsheet works if it is kept current. For each provider and payer, track: application type, date submitted, reference number, payer contact, current status, next follow-up date, effective date once approved, and the re-credentialing due date. Add a separate tab for expirations (licenses, DEA, malpractice, board certification) with reminders 90 and 30 days out. Review the tracker weekly with practice leadership, and tell billing the moment an effective date is confirmed.

## Skills to hire for

- Organization and deadline tracking across many payers and providers
- Experience with payer enrollment portals and shared credentialing databases
- Accuracy with detail-heavy forms
- Persistent, documented follow-up
- Coordination with providers, billing and practice leadership

## Staffing credentialing

Credentialing workload is lumpy. It spikes when you add providers, locations or payers, then settles into maintenance. Options:

- **In-house:** works when you hire often and want control. The risk is that it lands on an office manager with no time.
- **Credentialing service or billing company:** often priced per provider or per application.
- **Dedicated remote specialist:** works in your portals and tracker, part-time or full-time. This is useful for billing companies that credential for many clients.

Credentialing files include sensitive provider information (such as Social Security numbers and license details), so require secure access and clear data-handling rules whichever option you choose. If remote staff will also see patient data, sign a BAA first ([HIPAA guide](/hipaa-outsourced-medical-billing/)).

Front-end steps such as [prior authorization](/prior-authorization-specialist/) and [eligibility verification](/eligibility-verification-prior-authorization/) depend on providers being correctly enrolled, so connect credentialing to those teams.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between credentialing and payer enrollment?

Credentialing verifies a provider's qualifications. Enrollment adds the provider to a payer's network or program with an effective date, so claims can be paid.

### How long does credentialing take?

It varies widely by payer, state and application type. Start well before a provider's first day and ask each payer for its current processing expectations.

### What documents does a credentialing specialist need?

Typically licenses, DEA registration where applicable, board certification, malpractice insurance, CV and work history, and identifiers such as the NPI.

### Can credentialing be done remotely?

Yes. Most work happens in payer portals, credentialing databases and email. Require secure access and clear data-handling rules because the files contain sensitive provider information.

### Why do claims deny for credentialing reasons?

Common causes are a provider not yet enrolled with the payer, a wrong effective date, or the provider not linked to the correct group, tax ID or location.
