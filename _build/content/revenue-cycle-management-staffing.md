# Revenue cycle management staffing: roles, workflow, KPIs and costs

*Last reviewed: October 18, 2026.*

Revenue cycle management (RCM) is every step between a patient booking an appointment and your practice collecting the last dollar for that visit. When one step is understaffed, the effect shows up later, as denials, aging A/R and write-offs. This guide covers the RCM workflow, the roles behind it, the KPIs worth tracking and the three main ways practices and billing companies staff it: in-house, outsourced and dedicated remote or offshore teams.

## What RCM staffing means

RCM staffing is the work of putting trained people on each part of the revenue cycle. For a solo practice, that might be one person juggling everything. For a multi-location group or a billing company, it is a team with defined queues: eligibility, authorizations, coding, charge entry, claims, denials, A/R and posting.

There are three common models:

1. **In-house staff.** You recruit, employ, train and manage billing staff locally.
2. **Outsourced RCM.** A billing or RCM company takes over the function, typically under a contract priced as a percentage of collections or per claim. You trade control for convenience.
3. **Dedicated remote or offshore RCM staff.** A staffing provider recruits and employs staff, often in another country, who work full time in *your* systems and follow *your* processes. You keep control of the workflow and payer relationships.

Many organizations mix models, for example keeping a local billing manager and coder while adding remote staff for eligibility, posting and A/R follow-up.

## The RCM workflow, step by step

### 1. Eligibility and benefits verification

Before the visit, staff confirm active coverage, plan type, copays, deductibles, coordination of benefits and whether a referral or authorization is required. Errors here cause problems later: in a 2025 vendor survey of billing professionals, coordination of benefits issues were the most commonly cited denial reason (45% of respondents), followed by non-coverage (39%) ([Tebra](https://www.tebra.com/theintake/getting-paid/medical-billing-pain-points-insights-solutions)). Read more in our [eligibility verification and prior authorization guide](/eligibility-verification-prior-authorization/).

### 2. Prior authorization

Staff submit and track authorization requests, attach clinical documentation and follow up on pending decisions. The AMA's survey of 1,000 physicians found practices complete an average of 39 prior authorization requests per physician per week, and that 40% of physicians have staff who work exclusively on prior authorizations ([AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/prior-authorization/fixing-prior-auth-nearly-40-prior-authorizations-week-way)). Under the CMS Interoperability and Prior Authorization final rule, impacted payers (Medicare Advantage, Medicaid and CHIP fee-for-service and managed care; FFE qualified health plans are excluded from this particular timeframe requirement) must send decisions within 72 hours for expedited requests and seven calendar days for standard requests, starting in 2026, and must give a specific reason for denials ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/cms-interoperability-and-prior-authorization-final-rule-cms-0057-f)).

### 3. Medical coding

Certified coders assign CPT, HCPCS and ICD-10-CM codes from provider documentation. Code sets change on a schedule: ICD-10-CM updates generally take effect October 1 each year ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coding-billing/icd-10-codes)), and the AMA's CPT Editorial Panel maintains CPT ([AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/cpt/cpt-overview-and-code-approval)). Coding is skilled work; remote coders should hold recognized credentials and specialty experience.

### 4. Charge entry

Charges, modifiers, units, place of service and rendering provider are entered into the practice management system, often with a charge review step to catch missing documentation or mismatched codes before claims go out.

### 5. Claims submission and scrubbing

Claims are scrubbed for errors, submitted through a clearinghouse, and rejections (claims the payer or clearinghouse never accepted) are corrected quickly. Timely filing limits matter: Medicare generally requires claims within one calendar year of the date of service ([CMS Medicare Claims Processing Manual, Ch. 1, §70](https://www.cms.gov/regulations-and-guidance/guidance/manuals/downloads/clm104c01.pdf)), and commercial payer limits are often shorter.

### 6. Denial management

Denied claims are categorized by reason, corrected, resubmitted or appealed, and the root cause is fed back to the front end. HealthCare.gov insurers denied 20% of in-network claims in 2023 according to KFF's analysis of federal data, and consumers appealed fewer than 1% of denied claims ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/private-insurance/claims-denials-and-appeals-in-aca-marketplace-plans-in-2023/)). That data covers one market segment, but it shows why someone needs to own the denial queue. See [denial management basics](/denial-management/).

### 7. A/R follow-up

A/R specialists work unpaid claims by age and dollar value, check status on payer portals, call payers and escalate. The goal is to resolve claims before they age past timely filing or appeal limits. See [how to reduce AR days](/reduce-ar-days/).

### 8. Payment posting

Payments from ERAs (835 files), paper EOBs, lockbox deposits and patient payments are posted and reconciled to the bank. Accurate posting is how you spot underpayments, incorrect adjustments and denials that arrive inside a "paid" remittance.

### Supporting functions

- **Credentialing and payer enrollment**, so providers can bill payers at all.
- **Patient statements and patient A/R.**
- **Reporting**, so the practice sees the KPIs below every month.

## RCM KPIs, defined

Definitions vary slightly between organizations. Pick one definition, document it, and use it consistently.

- **Days in A/R:** total accounts receivable divided by average daily charges (for example, total charges over the last 90 days divided by 90). It estimates how long, on average, it takes to collect.
- **Clean claim rate:** the share of claims accepted and processed on first submission without edits, rejections or requests for more information.
- **Denial rate:** denied claims (or denied dollars) divided by total claims (or dollars) submitted in a period. Track by payer and reason code.
- **Net collection rate:** payments collected divided by the amount you were contractually entitled to collect (charges minus contractual adjustments) for the period. It shows how much collectible revenue you actually captured.
- **First-pass resolution rate:** the share of claims paid in full, or otherwise fully resolved, after the first submission with no further touches.
- **A/R over 90 (or 120) days:** the share of A/R older than 90 or 120 days.

**About benchmarks:** published benchmarks vary widely by specialty, payer mix and methodology, and many come from member-only surveys or vendor marketing. We don't publish a benchmark number unless we can cite a public source. Instead, trend your own numbers monthly and compare with specialty data you trust. ## In-house vs outsourced vs dedicated remote RCM: how to compare costs

We won't give you invented price ranges. Here is how to build a fair comparison with your own numbers.

**In-house cost** is more than salary. Add wages, payroll taxes, benefits, paid leave, recruiting, training, software seats, equipment, workspace and management time. Public data help as a starting point: the BLS reports a May 2025 median hourly wage of $23.32 for billing and posting clerks ([BLS OEWS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ocwage.t01.htm)) and a median annual wage of $51,140 for medical records specialists, the BLS category that includes medical coders ([BLS OOH](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/medical-records-and-health-information-technicians.htm)). Benefits add materially on top: in private industry, benefits averaged 30.0% of employer compensation costs in June 2026 ([BLS ECEC](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.nr0.htm)). Then add the cost of vacancies, because unworked queues keep aging while you hire.

**Outsourced RCM cost** is usually a percentage of collections, a per-claim fee, or a hybrid. Ask what is included (coding? credentialing? patient statements? appeals?), how "collections" is defined, minimums, setup fees and exit terms. Model the fee against your actual monthly collections.

**Dedicated remote or offshore staff** are usually priced per full-time staff member per month or per hour. Compare that rate against fully loaded in-house cost for the same role, then add your own costs: management time, onboarding, software seats and secure remote access (for example, a VDI setup).

**Non-cost factors** matter as much as price: control of the workflow, visibility into queues, turnover risk, coverage during absences, HIPAA safeguards, and how quickly you can add or remove capacity. ## Choosing an RCM staffing partner

Whatever model you pick, ask:

- Which roles can you staff, and what is the experience level for my specialty?
- Will staff be dedicated to us, and who supervises them day to day?
- How do you handle HIPAA: BAA, access controls, training, incident response? (See our [HIPAA guide](/hipaa-outsourced-medical-billing/).)
- How will we measure performance, and what reporting do we get?
- What happens if a staff member leaves?
- What are the contract length, notice period and exit terms?

Be wary of any vendor that guarantees a specific reduction in denials or A/R days before seeing your data. Results depend on your payers, specialty, documentation and starting point.

## Where a remote RCM team fits

A dedicated remote team tends to fit when you have steady, queue-based work (eligibility, posting, A/R, denials), systems that support secure remote access, and someone in-house who can own the process. It fits less well if your processes are undocumented or your billing system can't support remote access securely.
