# Hiring remote medical coders: what to verify, test and set up

*By the Medical Billing Team editors · Published October 18, 2026 · Updated October 18, 2026 · [How we research and review](/about/#how-we-write-our-guides)*

**Short answer:** When you outsource medical coding to remote coders, hire them the way you'd hire local ones, then add security controls. Verify credentials directly with the issuing body, match specialty experience to your services, and give a practical coding test on fictional records. Agree on accuracy audits and turnaround, and put a BAA, MFA, secure remote access and role-based access in place before the coder sees a chart.

Coding sits between documentation and payment. A coder who doesn't know your specialty, or who works without quality checks, can create denials, compliance risk and rework. This guide covers what to look for in a remote or offshore coder and how to set up the role safely. It's part of our [revenue cycle management staffing](/revenue-cycle-management-staffing/) guide.

## What a medical coder does

Medical coders review clinical documentation and assign diagnosis and procedure codes: ICD-10-CM for diagnoses, and CPT and HCPCS for procedures, services and supplies. Typical tasks include:

- coding encounters, procedures or facility claims from provider documentation;
- applying modifiers and checking payer-specific coding rules;
- sending queries to providers when documentation is unclear or incomplete;
- working coding-related denials with the billing team; and
- keeping up with annual and quarterly code-set changes.

Code sets change on a schedule. CMS notes that ICD-10 code updates take effect on October 1 each year ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/coding-billing/icd-10-codes)), and CPT is maintained by the AMA's CPT Editorial Panel ([AMA](https://www.ama-assn.org/practice-management/cpt/cpt-overview-and-code-approval)). Whoever codes for you needs a routine for updates.

## What it costs to hire locally

The Bureau of Labor Statistics groups coders with medical records specialists. It reports a median wage of $51,140 a year ($24.59 an hour) in May 2025 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/medical-records-and-health-information-technicians.htm)). Add benefits, which average 30% of private-industry compensation costs ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ecec.nr0.htm)), plus software, training and management time. ## Skills and credentials to verify

- **Credentials.** Recognized coding credentials from established certifying organizations. Verify them directly with the certifying body; don't rely on a résumé or a screenshot.
- **Specialty fit.** Professional (physician) coding differs from facility, ASC, behavioral health or therapy coding. Ask for experience in your services specifically.
- **Documentation literacy.** Can the coder spot when documentation doesn't support a code, and write a clear, compliant provider query?
- **Payer rules.** Modifier use, bundling edits and payer-specific policies.
- **Systems.** Your EHR and practice management system, encoder tools and any computer-assisted coding software you use.
- **Communication.** Clear written English for queries and notes.

## How to test a coding candidate

1. **Use fictional or de-identified sample notes.** Never use real patient records in a hiring test.
2. **Score accuracy, not speed alone.** Compare the candidate's codes with an answer key prepared by an experienced coder.
3. **Include one ambiguous case.** You're looking for a good provider query, not a guess.
4. **Include a denial.** Ask how they'd respond to a sample coding-related denial.

## Setting up the role

- **Scope.** Which encounter types, providers or locations, and what turnaround?
- **Quality program.** Agree on a regular audit sample, how errors are fed back, and what happens when accuracy drops. Many organizations audit new coders more heavily at first.
- **Query workflow.** How queries reach providers, how answers are documented, and who follows up.
- **Escalation.** Questions about compliance or medical necessity go to a named person in your organization.
- **Productivity.** Set expectations after a few weeks of real data, not before.

## Security and HIPAA for remote coders

Coders see full clinical documentation, so they're often the most PHI-exposed people on a billing team. Before access:

- a signed business associate agreement with the agency or contractor ([HHS](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/business-associates/index.html));
- unique logins with MFA, and secure remote access through a virtual desktop or VPN;
- no local downloads, printing or USB storage;
- access limited to the charts and functions the role needs; and
- documented HIPAA training.

## In-house, agency or freelancer?

A freelancer can cover a small, steady volume, but you handle vetting, backup coverage and security. An agency or staffing provider handles employment, equipment, backup coverage and the BAA, and adds a margin. A full-service billing company may include coding in a percentage-of-collections fee. Compare total cost and control, not just the hourly rate.

## FAQ

### Do remote medical coders need certification?

No federal law requires a coding credential, but many employers and agencies require one from an established certifying organization. Verify any credential directly with the certifying body.

### How do I test a remote coder?

Give a practical test using fictional or de-identified notes, score accuracy against an answer key prepared by an experienced coder, and include an ambiguous case to see how the candidate writes a provider query.

### Can offshore coders code for US practices?

Yes, if they're trained in US code sets and payer rules, work under a BAA with strong access controls, and your payer and Medicaid contracts don't restrict offshore access to member data.

### How often should a coder's work be audited?

Agree on a regular audit sample with your coding lead. New coders are commonly audited more heavily at first, then on a routine schedule once accuracy is consistent.

### What does a medical coder earn in the US?

BLS reports a median of $51,140 a year ($24.59 an hour) in May 2025 for medical records specialists, the occupation group that includes coders.
